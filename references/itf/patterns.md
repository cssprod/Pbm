# The 24 ITF Patterns (Tul 틀): Meanings, Heroes and Kid-Friendly Stories

Each pattern is a set sequence of movements against imaginary opponents. General Choi named every pattern after a hero, a legend or an event in Korean history. **The pattern is the plot.** Each entry below gives:

- **Official meaning**: a close paraphrase of the ITF definition students memorise (from the *Encyclopedia*).
- **Number facts**: what the movement count and diagram stand for.
- **History check**: where the official definition differs from mainstream history (for accuracy-minded writers).
- **Kid retelling**: a short story for ages 6-10, written for animation.
- **Episode hooks**: tenet pairing, moral, and safe visual ideas.
- **Avoid**: what not to show.

Movement counts follow the ITF *Encyclopedia*. Belt levels are the most common arrangement and vary between schools (see `belts.md`).

> **Why 24?** General Choi compared a human life (around 100 years) to a single day when measured against eternity. The 24 patterns stand for the 24 hours of one day, and so for a whole life.

## Coloured-belt patterns

### 1. Chon-Ji (천지) — 19 movements — 10th → 9th kup (white)

**Official meaning:** Chon-Ji means literally "the Heaven and the Earth". In the Orient it is interpreted as the creation of the world, or the beginning of human history. It is therefore the first pattern a beginner learns. It has two similar parts: one represents Heaven and the other the Earth.

**Diagram:** a plus sign ( ┼ ), "cross" shaped.

**Kid retelling: "The First Morning."** Long, long ago there was no up and no down, only swirling mist. Then the mist split in two. The light part floated up and became the **Sky**, wide and bright. The heavy part sank down and became the **Earth**, solid and strong. Between them, the very first sunrise appeared, and the world began. When you do Chon-Ji, the first half is the Sky (light, reaching) and the second half is the Earth (solid, grounded). Every journey begins with a first step, and every black belt once stood exactly where you are, at the very beginning.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Courtesy (bowing in, learning how to be a student). Moral: everyone is a beginner once. Visual: the hero's white belt glows as sky and earth form around them.

**History check:** Not a historical event. "Heaven and Earth" is a classic East Asian cosmology idea (also linked to the trigrams on the Korean flag).

### 2. Dan-Gun (단군) — 21 movements — 8th kup (yellow)

**Official meaning:** Named after the holy **Dan-Gun**, the legendary founder of Korea in the year **2333 B.C.**

**Fun fact:** In Dan-Gun every punch is aimed at **high section**, which many instructors explain as Dan-Gun climbing a mountain (or reaching for heaven). This is a school explanation, not in the official definition.

**The myth (from the *Samguk Yusa*, 13th century):** Hwan-In, Lord of Heaven, had a son, **Hwan-Ung**, who longed to live on Earth. Hwan-Ung came down to Mount Taebaek under a sacred sandalwood tree with 3,000 followers and the Lords of **Wind, Rain and Clouds**. He taught people farming, medicine, laws and the difference between right and wrong. A **bear** and a **tiger** came to him, begging to become human. He gave them a bundle of mugwort (a bitter herb) and 20 cloves of garlic and said: "Eat only this, and stay out of the sunlight in a cave for 100 days." The tiger grew restless and ran off. The bear stayed patient, and after 21 days she became a woman, **Ung-Nyeo**. She prayed under the sandalwood tree for a child. Hwan-Ung married her and their son was **Dan-Gun Wang-Geom**, who founded the first Korean kingdom, **Go-Joseon**, in 2333 BC.

**Kid retelling: "The Bear Who Waited."** A grumpy tiger and a gentle bear both wanted the same wish. The prince from the sky gave them a challenge: stay in a dark cave and eat only bitter garlic and spicy herbs. "Ugh! Boring!" roared the tiger, and after a few days he stomped out into the sunshine. The bear was bored too. Her tummy rumbled. But she counted the days, sang to herself, and kept going. On day 21 a warm light filled the cave, and her wish came true. Her son, Dan-Gun, grew up to build Korea's very first kingdom. *Note: the number 21 is the number of days in the myth AND the number of moves in the pattern Dan-Gun! (The connection is a fun coincidence the show can point out; the ITF definition doesn't claim it.)*

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Perseverance**. Moral: being patient is a superpower. Visual: tiger vs bear "comedy duo" in the cave, a day-counter on the cave wall, sky-prince descending with wind/rain/cloud spirits. **Great pilot episode.**

**History check:** A national founding myth, not literal history. Korea celebrates Dan-Gun on **Gaecheonjeol** ("National Foundation Day"), 3 October.

### 3. Do-San (도산) — 24 movements — 7th kup (yellow/green)

**Official meaning:** Do-San is the pseudonym of the patriot **Ahn Chang-Ho** (1876–1938). The 24 movements represent his entire life, which he devoted to furthering the education of Korea and its independence movement.

**Real story:** Ahn Chang-Ho built schools in Korea, and in 1902 went to **San Francisco** and later Los Angeles and **Riverside, California**, where he organized Korean immigrants. A well-known story: when he saw Korean immigrant homes looking messy and the neighbours looking down on them, he didn't lecture anyone. He quietly went house to house cleaning yards, planting flowers and fixing curtains, until the whole community took pride in itself and earned respect. He taught that every person should be truthful and do their part ("**Let us be truthful**"; his idea of *mu-sil yeok-haeng*, "sincere effort, real action"). He was imprisoned several times for working for Korean independence and died in 1938. There is a statue of him in Riverside, California, and an interchange there is named after him.

**Kid retelling: "The Boy Who Cleaned the Street."** When Chang-Ho moved to America, he noticed people from his country felt sad and far from home. Their houses were messy, and some neighbours were unkind. Chang-Ho didn't shout or complain. He picked up a broom. Day after day, he swept porches, planted flowers and fixed fences. Soon others joined in. The street became the nicest on the block, and the neighbours started to say hello. "One person doing the right thing," he said, "can change a whole town."

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Integrity** (or Courtesy). Moral: lead by example, learning is power. Visual: a messy street transforming in a montage. Strong tie-in for American and diaspora kids.

**Avoid:** prison or torture details.

### 4. Won-Hyo (원효) — 28 movements — 6th kup (green)

**Official meaning:** Won-Hyo was the noted monk who introduced Buddhism to the Silla Dynasty in the year **686 A.D.**

**Diagram:** usually drawn as an "I" shape (check against the *Encyclopedia* before animating floor lines).

**History check:** Buddhism had already become Silla's official religion in 527 AD. Won-Hyo (617–686) made it popular with ordinary people. **686 is the year he died.** For the show: "Won-Hyo helped share Buddhism with everyday people in Silla."

**The famous legend (the skull cup):** Won-Hyo and his friend Ui-Sang set out for China to study. One stormy night they slept in a cave. Waking up thirsty in the dark, Won-Hyo found a bowl of water and drank. It was the sweetest, coolest water he'd ever tasted. In the morning he saw the "bowl" was an old skull, and he felt sick. Then he realized: the water hadn't changed, only his thoughts about it had. *Everything depends on the mind.* He decided he didn't need to travel far to find wisdom, and went home to teach.

**Kid retelling: "The Sweetest Water."** (Softened: use an old cracked bowl covered in moss, or a "spooky old bowl".) On a rainy night, Won-Hyo drank from a bowl in a dark cave. Delicious! In the morning he saw it was a yucky, mossy, spider-webby old pot. "Eww!" But then he laughed. "The water was the same. Only my thinking changed!" Won-Hyo learned that how we *think* about something changes how we *feel* about it. He went home and taught everyone, singing and dancing in the streets so even kids could understand.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Self-Control** (of your mind and attitude). Moral: your thoughts are powerful, so choose them wisely. Great for "I can't do it" → "I can't do it *yet*." Visual: Won-Hyo dancing in a village with a gourd.

**Avoid:** realistic skulls (keep it a spooky pot/gourd for this age group). Avoid religious preaching; keep it about mindset.

### 5. Yul-Gok (율곡) — 38 movements — 5th kup (green/blue)

**Official meaning:** Yul-Gok is the pseudonym of the great philosopher and scholar **Yi I** (1536–1584), nicknamed the "**Confucius of Korea**". The 38 movements refer to his birthplace on the **38th degree latitude**, and the diagram (士) represents "**scholar**".

**Real story:** Yi I's mother was **Shin Saimdang**, one of Korea's most famous artists. Both appear on Korean money (he is on the 5,000 won note; she is on the 50,000 won note, the only mother and son on the same country's currency). He is said to have passed the state examinations in first place nine times. He warned the king to train **100,000 soldiers** to prepare for a possible invasion. The king didn't listen, and Japan invaded in 1592, eight years after Yi I died.

**Kid retelling: "The Boy Who Learned from His Mom."** Little Yi I loved watching his mother paint. She painted grasshoppers so real that, people said, chickens tried to peck them! She taught him to look closely and keep learning. He became the best student in all of Korea, top of the class *nine times*. But he was most proud of thinking ahead. "We should get ready now, before trouble comes," he told the king.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Perseverance** (studying hard) plus being prepared. Moral: learning never stops; prepare before you need to. Visual: Saimdang's painted bugs coming to life (great animation moment). Good Mother's Day episode.

**History check:** "Passed exams first nine times" is the traditional story (*Gu-do jang-won-gong*). The 100,000-soldier proposal is traditional; some historians debate it.

### 6. Joong-Gun (중근) — 32 movements — 4th kup (blue)

**Official meaning:** Joong-Gun is named after the patriot **An Joong-Gun**, who assassinated **Hiro-Bumi Ito**, the first Japanese governor-general of Korea, known as the man who played the leading part in the Korea-Japan merger. The 32 movements represent Mr. An's age when he was executed at Lui-Shung prison (1910).

> ⚠️ **Most sensitive pattern for kids.** The official definition is about an assassination. **Never depict the shooting.** Focus on his love of his country, his bravery in standing up to injustice, his calligraphy and his writing on peace.

**History check:** He was 30 by Western counting (born 1879) and about 32 by traditional Korean age counting. The event was at Harbin station, 26 October 1909. He was executed at Lushun (Port Arthur), 26 March 1910. Japanese governance of Korea: Ito was Resident-General.

**Safe facts:** While in prison he wrote beautiful **calligraphy**, which is now treasured in Korea, and began a book called *On Peace in East Asia*, imagining Korea, China and Japan as peaceful partners sharing a bank and an army for peace. Even his Japanese guards admired him and asked for his calligraphy. He famously stamped his works with his **left handprint, missing the top of one finger**: he had cut it off as a sign of his promise to free Korea. *(Skip the finger detail for under-8s.)*

**Kid retelling: "The Brush and the Promise."** Joong-Gun loved Korea with all his heart. When Korea lost its freedom, he made a promise to help his country be free again, and he kept it, even though it was very dangerous and cost him everything. In his last days, sitting in a small cell, he did something surprising: he picked up a brush and wrote beautiful words about **peace**, hoping that one day all the countries nearby would be friends. Even the guards were amazed and asked him for his writing.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Indomitable Spirit**. Moral: keep your promises; even in hard times, dream of peace. Suggest aiming this at the older end of the audience (8-10) with a parent note.

### 7. Toi-Gye (퇴계) — 37 movements — 3rd kup (blue/red)

**Official meaning:** Toi-Gye is the pen name of the noted scholar **Yi Hwang** (16th century), an authority on neo-Confucianism. The 37 movements refer to his birthplace on the **37th degree latitude**, and the diagram (士) represents "**scholar**".

**Real story:** Yi Hwang (1501–1570) is on Korea's **1,000 won note**. He refused high government jobs again and again because he preferred to teach. He started the **Dosan Seowon** academy. He was famous for being humble: he answered letters from young scholars as if they were equals, and once happily changed his mind in a long letter debate with a much younger scholar (Gi Dae-Seung) when he decided the younger man had made good points.

**Kid retelling: "The Teacher Who Listened."** Yi Hwang was the smartest man in Korea. Kings wanted him at the palace, but he said, "No thank you, I'd rather teach." One day a young student wrote to him: "I think you're wrong." Instead of getting angry, Yi Hwang thought about it for a long, long time. Then he wrote back: "You know what? You have a good point. Thank you." The greatest teacher was also the greatest listener.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Courtesy** (humility). Moral: smart people listen and can admit they're wrong. Visual: letters flying back and forth between mountains like paper birds.

### 8. Hwa-Rang (화랑) — 29 movements — 2nd kup (red)

**Official meaning:** Hwa-Rang is named after the **Hwa-Rang youth group**, which originated in the Silla Dynasty in the early 7th century. The 29 movements refer to the **29th Infantry Division**, where Taekwon-Do developed into maturity.

**Real story:** Hwa-Rang means "**Flower Youth**" or "Flowering Knights". They were teenage boys from noble families in Silla who trained together in martial arts, but also in **poetry, music, dance and good manners**. They travelled to mountains and rivers to grow strong and wise. The monk **Won-Gwang** gave them **five rules** (*Sesok Ogye*):
1. Be loyal to your country (king).
2. Respect your parents.
3. Be trustworthy to your friends.
4. Never retreat in battle (*kid version: don't give up*).
5. Never take a life without good reason (*kid version: be gentle with living things; only use force when you truly must*).

Many people consider the Hwa-Rang an inspiration for modern Korean martial arts, and General Choi drew on their ideals. Hwa-Rang is the series' natural "**team of heroes**" template.

**Kid retelling: "The Flower Knights."** Long ago in Silla, there was a special club for kids. They didn't just learn to kick and jump. They learned to sing, write poems, climb mountains and be kind. Before they could join, they had to promise five things: love your home, respect your parents, be a true friend, never give up, and never hurt any living thing without a very good reason. The Flower Knights were strong *and* gentle, like a flower growing out of a rock.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: all five. Moral: true strength includes kindness and creativity. Visual: kid-knights on a mountain, playing flutes and practising kicks. Could be the name/idea of the in-show kid club. Also connects back to General Choi's 29th Division.

**Avoid:** the famous story of the boy Hwa-Rang Gwan-Chang dying in battle at 16.

### 9. Choong-Moo (충무) — 30 movements — 1st kup (red/black)

**Official meaning:** Choong-Moo was the name given to the great **Admiral Yi Soon-Sin** of the Yi Dynasty. He was reputed to have invented the first armoured battleship (**Kobukson**, the "turtle ship") in 1592, which is said to be the precursor of the present-day submarine. The pattern ends with a **left-hand attack** to symbolize his regrettable death, having no chance to show his unrestrained potentiality, checked by the forced reservation of his loyalty to the king.

**History check:** Yi improved and famously used the turtle ship; earlier versions are recorded around 1413. "Precursor of the submarine" is ITF tradition, not a historical claim. His title "Chungmu" (loyalty-and-martial-valour) was given after his death. He died in the Battle of Noryang (1598).

**Real story:** Admiral Yi never lost a sea battle. His most famous moment was the **Battle of Myeongnyang (1597)**: after the navy had been nearly destroyed (while he had been unfairly removed from command), he had just **13 ships against more than 130**. He told the king: "I still have twelve ships" (a thirteenth joined later), and he won by cleverly using the narrow strait's powerful tides. He also kept a famous diary, the *Nanjung Ilgi*. Before he died he said: "Don't tell anyone I'm gone, the battle isn't over yet," so his sailors would keep their courage.

**Kid retelling: "Thirteen Little Ships."** Admiral Yi was in trouble. The other side had more than 130 ships. He had only 13. Everyone said, "Run away!" But Yi studied the sea. He knew a narrow, twisty channel where the water rushed like a river, and he knew exactly when the tide would turn. He built amazing **turtle ships** with spiky shell roofs and dragon heads that puffed smoke. He waited for the right moment, and when the tide turned, his little fleet won. Brains, bravery and never giving up beat being the biggest.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Indomitable Spirit**. Moral: being outnumbered doesn't mean you'll lose; use your head. Visual: turtle ship (fan-favourite design), tides swirling. Also Admiral Yi was unfairly punished and still stayed loyal: a lesson on handling unfairness without anger (Self-Control).

**Avoid:** battle deaths, sinking crews.

## 1st Dan (black belt) patterns

### 10. Kwang-Gae (광개) — 39 movements — 1st Dan

**Official meaning:** Kwang-Gae is named after the famous **Kwang-Gae-Toh-Wang**, the 19th king of the Koguryo Dynasty, who regained all the lost territories, including the greater part of Manchuria. The diagram (土) represents the expansion and recovery of lost territory. The 39 movements refer to the first two figures of **391 A.D.**, the year he came to the throne.

**History check:** Usually counted as the 19th king of Goguryeo (r. 391–413). His name means "Great King, Broad Expander of Territory". His son raised a **6-metre-tall stone stele** (the Gwanggaeto Stele, 414 AD), still standing in China today, carved with his story.

**Kid retelling: "The Giant Stone Story."** A king's son wanted everyone to remember his dad forever, so he carved the story into a stone as tall as a giraffe! It tells of King Kwang-Gae, who became king at just 18 and worked to bring his people's lands back together. **Episode idea:** the kids find the stele and "read" the story as it comes to life.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Perseverance. Moral: your actions are the story you leave behind. **Avoid:** conquest glorification; present as "bringing his people's home back together".

### 11. Po-Eun (포은) — 36 movements — 1st Dan

**Official meaning:** Po-Eun is the pseudonym of **Chong Mong-Chu**, a loyal subject who was a famous poet. His poem "I would not serve a second master though I might be crucified a hundred times" is known to every Korean. He was also a pioneer in the field of physics. The diagram (—) represents his unerring loyalty to the king and country towards the end of the Koryo Dynasty.

**Real story:** Chong Mong-Chu (Jeong Mong-ju, 1337–1392) was also a diplomat and education reformer. When a powerful family was taking over the kingdom, their son Yi Bang-Won sang him a poem asking him to join them ("What does it matter if the vines tangle together?"). Chong answered with his famous poem, the **Dansimga** ("Song of a Loyal Heart"), saying that even if he died a hundred times, his loyal heart would never change.

**Kid retelling: "The Poem Battle."** Two men didn't fight with swords. They fought with **poems**! The first sang, "Let's be like vines, all tangled together. Join my side!" Po-Eun sang back, "Even if I lived and died a hundred times, my heart would stay true." He wouldn't change sides just because it was easier. That's loyalty.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Integrity**. Moral: be loyal and true to your word even under peer pressure. Visual: a magical "rap battle" of glowing poem-words. **Avoid:** his murder on the Seonjuk Bridge.

---

### 12. Ge-Baek (계백) — 44 movements — 1st Dan

**Official meaning:** Ge-Baek is named after **Ge-Baek**, a great general in the Baek-Je Dynasty (660 A.D.). The diagram ( | ) represents his severe and strict military discipline.

**Real story:** At the Battle of Hwangsanbeol (660), General Gyebaek led about **5,000 soldiers against 50,000**. His small army held firm for a long time thanks to discipline and teamwork.

**Kid retelling: "Five Thousand Strong."** General Ge-Baek's team was small, but every soldier knew their job, practised every day, and stood together like the fingers of one fist. When things got scary, nobody ran. *(Keep it about teamwork and discipline; stop the story before the outcome.)*

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Self-Control / discipline. Moral: practice and teamwork make a small team mighty. **Avoid:** the traditional story that he killed his own family before the battle. **Never include.**

## 2nd Dan patterns

### 13. Eui-Am (의암) — 45 movements — 2nd Dan

**Official meaning:** Eui-Am is the pseudonym of **Son Byong Hi**, leader of the Korean independence movement on **March 1, 1919**. The 45 movements refer to his age when he changed the name of Dong Hak (Oriental culture) to **Chondo Kyo** (Heavenly Way religion) in 1905. The diagram ( | ) represents his indomitable spirit, displayed while dedicating himself to the prosperity of his nation.

**Real story:** Son Byong-Hi led the 33 people who signed the Korean Declaration of Independence in 1919, which led to a huge **peaceful** protest across Korea. A kid-friendly link: his son-in-law, **Bang Jeong-Hwan**, created **Children's Day** in Korea (now a national holiday on 5 May) and was one of the first people to use the Korean word *eorini* to give children respect. *(Verify the Bang Jeong-Hwan link in sources before broadcast: S12.)*

**Kid retelling:** "A man who believed every person is precious, a little bit of heaven inside each of us. He led millions of people to ask for freedom peacefully, waving flags and singing instead of fighting."

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Indomitable Spirit. Moral: peaceful courage. Good Children's Day (5 May) tie-in.

---

### 14. Choong-Jang (충장) — 52 movements — 2nd Dan

**Official meaning:** Choong-Jang is the pseudonym given to **General Kim Duk Ryang**, who lived during the Yi Dynasty, 14th century. The pattern ends with a **left-hand attack** to symbolize the tragedy of his death at 27 in prison, before he was able to reach full maturity.

**History check:** Kim Deok-Ryeong (1567–1596) actually lived in the **16th** century and fought during the Imjin War (1592–98) as a leader of a righteous (volunteer) army. He was falsely accused of treason and died in prison aged about 29 (by Korean count). He was later cleared and honoured.

**Kid retelling:** "Kim Duk Ryang was a young hero who volunteered to protect his people. Some jealous people told lies about him. Even though it wasn't fair, people later learned the truth and remembered him as a hero." Lesson: lies can't change the truth forever.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Integrity. Moral: don't spread rumours; the truth comes out. **Avoid:** prison death details.

---

### 15a. Ko-Dang (고당) — 39 movements — 2nd Dan (original pattern; **recommended for this series**)

**Official meaning (older ITF):** Ko-Dang is the pseudonym of the patriot **Cho Man Sik**, who dedicated his life to the independence movement and education of his people. The 39 movements signify his times of imprisonment and his birthplace on the 39th parallel.

**Real story:** Cho Man-Sik (1883–1950?) is called the "**Gandhi of Korea**" because he believed in **non-violent** resistance. He led the **Korean Products Promotion Movement**: "Let's make our own things and use what we make." He wore simple homespun cotton clothes to set an example, and he built schools.

**Kid retelling: "The Man in Homemade Clothes."** Ko-Dang wore plain clothes his own people made, even though fancy ones were available. "If we make our own things and help each other, we'll be strong without ever throwing a punch," he said.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Self-Control, Indomitable Spirit. Moral: peaceful strength, self-reliance, supporting your community.

### 15b. Juche (주체) — 45 movements — 2nd Dan (current ITF in many groups)

**Official meaning:** Juche is a philosophical idea that man is the master of everything and decides everything; in other words, that man is the master of the world and his own destiny. It is said this idea was rooted in **Baekdu Mountain**, which symbolizes the spirit of the Korean people. The diagram represents Baekdu Mountain.

**Guidance:** Juche replaced Ko-Dang in the 1980s. The term is North Korea's state ideology. **Do not build an episode around Juche.** If needed, a neutral kid-safe angle: Baekdu Mountain (the volcano with **Heaven Lake** on top, a sacred mountain in Korean legend) and the idea "*you are in charge of your own choices*". Better: use Ko-Dang. See `general-choi.md`.

## 3rd Dan patterns

### 16. Sam-Il (삼일) — 33 movements — 3rd Dan

**Official meaning:** Sam-Il denotes the historical date of the independence movement of Korea, which began throughout the country on **March 1, 1919**. The 33 movements stand for the **33 patriots** who planned the movement.

**Real story:** "Sam-Il" means "three-one" (3rd month, 1st day). Millions of Koreans peacefully marched, shouting "Daehan Dongnip Manse!" ("Long live Korean independence!"). A teenage girl, **Yu Gwan-Sun**, about 16, organized marches in her home town and is remembered as a national hero. 1 March is a national holiday in Korea.

**Kid retelling: "The Day a Whole Country Sang."** On one spring day, people all over Korea, grown-ups and even kids, came out holding flags and calling out "Manse!" together. No one threw a punch. They just stood together and asked to be free. And a brave girl named Gwan-Sun, not much older than a big sister, helped lead her whole town.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Indomitable Spirit, Courtesy. Moral: peaceful courage; kids can be brave leaders. **Avoid:** violence against protesters, Yu Gwan-Sun's death in prison.

---

### 17. Yoo-Sin (유신) — 68 movements — 3rd Dan

**Official meaning:** Yoo-Sin is named after **General Kim Yoo Sin**, a commanding general during the Silla Dynasty. The 68 movements refer to the last two figures of **668 A.D.**, the year Korea was united. The ready posture signifies a sword drawn on the right rather than the left side, symbolizing Yoo-Sin's mistake of following his king's orders to fight with foreign forces against his own nation.

**Legend:** As a 17-year-old Hwa-Rang, Kim Yu-Sin went alone into a mountain cave to train and pray for the strength to protect his people. A mysterious old hermit appeared, tested his heart, and gave him a secret book of strategy (in some versions a magic sword lit by starlight).

**Kid retelling: "The Cave and the Old Hermit."** Teenage Yoo-Sin climbed a mountain to train alone. A strange old man appeared. "Why do you want to be strong?" he asked. "To protect people," said Yoo-Sin. The hermit smiled and gave him a secret gift, then vanished. Lesson: *why* you train matters more than how hard you hit.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Integrity. Also: everyone makes mistakes, even heroes (the right-side sword is the pattern "remembering" a mistake). A good lesson about owning mistakes. **Avoid:** the story where he beheads his horse.

---

### 18. Choi-Yong (최영) — 46 movements — 3rd Dan

**Official meaning:** Choi-Yong is named after **General Choi Yong**, premier and commander-in-chief of the armed forces during the 14th century Koryo Dynasty. Choi Yong was greatly respected for his loyalty, patriotism and humility. He was executed by his subordinate commanders headed by General Yi Sung Gae, who later became the first king of the Yi Dynasty.

**Famous teaching:** His father told him: "**Look upon gold as if it were a stone**" (*gyeon-geum yeo-seok* 견금여석). Choi Yong lived by this all his life. He became the most powerful general in the land but stayed simple and never got greedy.

**Kid retelling: "Gold Is Just a Rock."** Little Choi Yong's dad gave him one big rule: "If you ever see gold, think of it as just a stone." When Choi Yong grew up and became the most important general, people tried to give him gold and gifts so he'd do what they wanted. He just shrugged. "It's only a rock to me." No one could buy him.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Integrity**. Moral: don't let wanting stuff change who you are. Nice tie-in with a "found money" honesty plot, or with not being bribed by candy/toys. **Avoid:** his execution.

## 4th Dan patterns

### 19. Yon-Gae (연개) — 49 movements — 4th Dan

**Official meaning:** Yon-Gae is named after a famous general of the Koguryo Dynasty, **Yon Gae Somoon**. The 49 movements refer to the last two figures of **649 A.D.**, the year he forced the Tang Dynasty to quit Korea after destroying nearly 300,000 of their troops at Ansi Song.

**History check:** The famous defence of **Ansi Fortress** was in 645 AD, led by the fortress commander (traditionally named Yang Man-Chun); Yeon Gaesomun was Goguryeo's ruler-general at the time. Mainstream historians don't support the 300,000 figure. Use the ITF definition for the meaning; for story, use the fortress siege.

**Kid retelling: "The Fortress That Wouldn't Fall."** A giant army surrounded a little mountain fortress. Day after day, the defenders fixed every crack in the walls. The enemy even built a huge dirt hill to climb over, and the defenders captured it! After months, the giant army gave up and went home.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Perseverance. Moral: keep fixing, keep going. **Avoid:** casualty numbers.

### 20. Ul-Ji (을지) — 42 movements — 4th Dan

**Official meaning:** Ul-Ji is named after **General Ul-Ji Moon Dok**, who successfully defended Korea against a Tang invasion force of nearly one million soldiers led by Yang Je in 612 A.D. Ul-Ji, employing hit-and-run guerrilla tactics, was able to decimate a large percentage of the force. The diagram (L) represents his surname. The 42 movements represent the **age of the author** (General Choi) when he designed the pattern.

**History check:** The invader in 612 was the **Sui** dynasty (Emperor Yang), not Tang. The famous victory is the **Battle of Salsu River**.

**The famous poem:** Eulji Mundeok sent the enemy general (Yu Zhongwen) a cheeky poem: *"Your divine plans have plumbed the heavens; your clever schemes have mapped the earth. You've won every battle, and your fame is great, so why not be satisfied, and stop?"* He also pretended to lose small battles to lure the tired army far from home.

**Kid retelling: "The Cleverest General."** A huge army marched toward Korea. General Ul-Ji didn't panic. He let them chase him, a little further and a little further, until they were tired and hungry. Then he sent their general a funny poem: "You're SO amazing, you've won SO much... maybe it's time to go home now?" The big army realized they'd been tricked and turned around.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: **Self-Control**. Moral: think before you act; a clever mind beats big muscles; you can win without fighting. Also a meta-moment: "General Choi was 42 when he made this pattern!" **Avoid:** the drowning of the retreating army.

### 21. Moon-Moo (문무) — 61 movements — 4th Dan

**Official meaning:** Moon-Moo honours the **30th king of the Silla Dynasty**. His body was buried near **Dae Wang Am** (Great King's Rock). According to his will, the body was placed in the sea "*where my soul shall forever defend my land against the Japanese*." It is said that the **Sok Gul Am** (stone cave) was built to guard his tomb. The Sok Gul Am is a fine example of the culture of the Silla Dynasty. The 61 movements symbolize the last two figures of **661 A.D.**, when Moon Moo came to the throne.

**History check:** Seokguram Grotto was actually built in the 8th century and is not usually linked to his tomb; this is ITF tradition. Daewangam is a real rocky islet off Gyeongju's coast.

**The magical legend: the Flute that Calms Ten Thousand Waves (*Manpasikjeok*).** After King Munmu became a **sea dragon** to protect his land, his son was given a magic bamboo from a floating island, a gift from the dragon-king and the spirit of General Kim Yu-Sin. Made into a flute, when played it **calmed storms, stopped enemies, brought rain in droughts and healed the sick**.

**Kid retelling: "The Dragon King's Flute."** King Moon-Moo loved his kingdom so much that he said, "When I'm gone, put me in the sea. I'll become a dragon and guard our shores forever!" Years later, a strange island floated up with a single bamboo stalk that split in two by day and joined into one at night. The new king made it into a flute. Whenever he played it, storms calmed, waves went still, and everyone was safe.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Indomitable Spirit (protecting even after you're gone) and *harmony*: "two halves becoming one" fits the unity theme. **One of the best fantasy set pieces for the series** (dragon + magic flute). Avoid making Japan the villain; say "raiders from the sea".

## 5th Dan patterns

### 22. So-San (서산) — 72 movements — 5th Dan

**Official meaning:** So-San is the pseudonym of the great monk **Choi Hyong Ung** (1520–1604) during the Yi Dynasty. The 72 movements refer to his age when he organized a corps of monk soldiers with the assistance of his pupil **Sa Myung Dang**. The monk soldiers helped repulse the Japanese pirates who overran most of the Korean peninsula in 1592.

**History check:** Usually known as Seosan Daesa (birth name Choi Yeo-Sin; dharma name Hyujeong). The 1592 invasion was a full army invasion (the Imjin War), not just pirates.

**Kid retelling: "Grandpa Monk."** So-San was 72 years old, a grandpa who spent his days meditating in the mountains. When his country needed help, everyone said, "You're too old!" He smiled, picked up his walking stick and gathered thousands of monks to help protect the people. With his student, Sa-Myung, he showed that you're never too young or too old to help.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Indomitable Spirit. Moral: age doesn't matter, anyone can help; respect your elders (they may be the toughest of all!). Great grandparent-themed episode.

### 23. Se-Jong (세종) — 24 movements — 5th Dan

**Official meaning:** Se-Jong is named after the greatest Korean king, **Se-Jong**, who invented the Korean alphabet in **1443**, and was also a noted meteorologist. The diagram represents the king, while the 24 movements refer to the **24 letters of the Korean alphabet**.

**Real story:** King Sejong (r. 1418–1450) saw that ordinary people couldn't read, because writing used thousands of difficult Chinese characters. He created **Hangul**, a simple alphabet "so easy that a wise person can learn it before the morning is over, and even a slow learner in ten days." It was announced in 1446 (celebrated as **Hangul Day, 9 October**). His team also built the world's first standardized **rain gauge** (1441) with inventor **Jang Yeong-Sil**, plus water clocks and sundials. Hangul originally had 28 letters; 24 are used today.

**Kid retelling: "The King Who Gave Everyone Letters."** King Sejong noticed something sad: most people in his kingdom couldn't read or write. The old way of writing had thousands and thousands of complicated characters. "That's not fair," he said. So he worked and worked, getting sick from studying late at night, until he invented a brand-new alphabet so simple that a kid could learn it in a few days. Some letters are even shaped like your mouth when you say them!

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Perseverance, Courtesy (caring about everyone). Moral: use your power to help everyone, and learning should be for all. Visual: Hangul letters dancing out of mouths and throats; a mini "learn 3 Korean letters" segment. Also the inventor Jang Yeong-Sil as a sidekick for gadget episodes.

## 6th Dan pattern

### 24. Tong-Il (통일) — 56 movements — 6th Dan

**Official meaning:** Tong-Il denotes the resolution of the **unification of Korea**, which has been divided since 1945. The diagram ( | ) symbolizes the homogeneous race.

**Kid retelling (keep it apolitical): "Two Halves of One Family."** Sometimes families get separated: by distance, by arguments, or by big events in the world. In Korea, many families were separated a long time ago and still miss each other. Tong-Il means "**becoming one again**." General Choi made it the very last pattern because he hoped for one big family coming back together.

**Episode hooks:** Tenet: Courtesy, Indomitable Spirit. Moral: making up after a fight; forgiveness; bringing people together. **Series finale material**: the hero brings two rival groups of friends together. Mirror with Chon-Ji: the first pattern is about one becoming two (heaven and earth), and the last is about two becoming one. **Avoid:** any North/South political content or leaders.
